<CheatRule label="The single most important idea">
  A language model is a <Highlight>probability distribution over sequences of words</Highlight>. The simple task of predicting the next word, scaled enough, turned out to be powerful enough to do almost any NLP problem — that is why the whole field reorganised around it.
</CheatRule>

<Image
  src="/images/blog/nlp-from-scratch/language-modeling/cheatsheet-page-3-rnn-elmo.png"
  alt="Page 3 of 4. Ten cards: (21) why n-grams are not enough — fixed window misses long-range subject-verb agreement ('The keys to the old cabinet ... are missing'); (22) recurrent neural networks — read one word at a time, carry a hidden state, RNN cells chained left to right; (23) how an RNN language model works — at each step t use current embedding x_t plus previous hidden state h_{t-1} to produce h_t and softmax over the next word; (24) RNN/LSTM payoff — perplexity drops from 5-gram 182 to improved n-gram 143 to LSTM 84; (25) RNN/LSTM limitations — still struggle with long-range dependencies, vanishing gradients, sequential and non-parallelisable; (26) ELMo contextual embeddings — internal hidden states from a bidirectional LSTM used as word embeddings; (27) ELMo fixes polysemy — 'I deposited money at the bank' vs 'We sat by the river bank' yield different contextual vectors; (28) how we use ELMo — feed the bidirectional LM's internal representations into downstream classifiers, pretrain once and reuse; (29) transformers vs RNNs — no recurrence, direct token-to-token access, parallelisable, O(1) path length; (30) self-attention — every word looks at every other word ('it' attends to 'animal'), attention weights sum to 1."

<CheatRule label="Final exam traps" accent="red">

1. **A language model is just a probability distribution over sequences.** Everything else is engineering on top of that one idea. Do not let the architecture diagrams distract you.
2. **N-grams fail on long-distance dependencies, but not local ones.** They are not useless — for many small applications a trigram still works.
3. **Lower perplexity is better.** Easy to flip on a stressed exam. Lower means the model assigned high probability to the test set.
4. **Perplexity comparisons are only valid on the same test set.** Different test sets give different absolute numbers.
5. **ELMo is used for its hidden states, not its predictions.** The output softmax is irrelevant for downstream tasks — we yank out the internal layers as features.
6. **Self-attention is not the same as the attention used in seq2seq machine translation.** Self-attention takes only one input (the sentence) and asks each word to attend to every other word in the same sentence.
7. **BERT is bidirectional because the masked LM task lets it be.** A causal next-word task forces left-to-right; masking does not.
8. **GPT-3's zero-shot does not mean it has zero knowledge of the task.** It has read enough text in pretraining that it has seen the task pattern somewhere. Zero-shot means zero **labelled task-specific** examples at inference.
9. **Distillation works because big models are over-parameterised, not because they are wrong.** The student copies a smoothed function the teacher computes.

</CheatRule>

<CheatRule label="One-line summary" accent="navy">
  Predict the next word — well enough, on enough text, with enough parameters — and you accidentally learn the entire language. That is the <Highlight>core thesis of modern NLP</Highlight>, and language models are how we prove it.
</CheatRule>
