<CheatRule label="The single most important idea">
  Semantics is about <Highlight>senses, not words</Highlight>. We can either hand-build a database of senses and relationships (WordNet) or learn them from context counts (distributional → PMI → Word2Vec). The distributional approach wins because it scales — and accidentally encodes analogies as <Highlight>directions in vector space</Highlight>.
</CheatRule>

<CheatRule label="Final exam traps" accent="red">

1. **WordNet is not enough.** Coverage is partial, drift is real, verbs and adjectives are weak. You need word sense disambiguation before you can use it.
2. **Similarity is not synonymy.** Antonyms and co-hyponyms can have high similarity. Similarity rolls multiple relationships into one number.
3. **PPMI just thresholds PMI at 0.** That throws away the negative signal — which is mostly noise anyway, but be aware.
4. **Word2Vec does not understand language.** It predicts context. Everything else is emergent from compression.
5. **king − man + woman = queen is the byproduct.** The training objective is context prediction, not analogy.
6. **One vector per word is the limitation.** Polysemy, order, and domain are all broken until contextual embeddings (BERT, transformers) show up — that's Part 10.

</CheatRule>

<CheatRule label="One-line summary" accent="navy">
  Senses live in a vector space. WordNet hand-drew the map; PMI counted it; Word2Vec learned it from text alone — and discovered <Highlight>analogies as directions</Highlight>.
</CheatRule>
