*Machine Learning from Scratch · Part 5/12*

[Part 4](/blog/ml-from-scratch-04-classification-metrics) was about picking the right metric — the *language* you use to score a model. This post is about using that metric *honestly* — so the number you compute on your dataset actually predicts how the model will behave in production.

A model can score 99% accuracy on your laptop and 75% in production. Not because the model is bad — because the *evaluation* was unreliable. Validation is the discipline of making sure that doesn't happen.

This post covers:

1. The four kinds of model problem — **bias, variance, overfitting, nonsignificance** — and how to recognise them.
2. The **bias-variance trade-off** — the U-shape that decides the quality of every model.
3. Why a single train/test split is a **coin flip**, and how **k-fold cross-validation** fixes it.
4. The **3-way train / validation / test split** for hyperparameter tuning, and why you **retrain on all the data** afterwards.
5. **Bootstrap** for small samples and confidence intervals.
6. **Significance testing** — is the improvement real or noise?
7. **Distribution drift** — what changes in production that the test set can't catch.

## Four common model problems

Before we get to validation tools, it helps to know what we're validating *against*. From my notes, there are four distinct ways a model can fail — and not every bad result means the same thing.

- <KeyTerm>Bias</KeyTerm> — *systematic error*. The model is too simple to capture the pattern in the data. It misses the main signal.
- <KeyTerm>Variance</KeyTerm> — *oversensitivity*. The model reacts too strongly to small fluctuations in the training data. Tiny changes in the input produce wildly different predictions.
- <KeyTerm>Overfit</KeyTerm> — features the model learned that exist in the *training* data but don't generalise to the population. The cousin of high variance.
- <KeyTerm>Nonsignificance</KeyTerm> — the model was built on an assumed relationship that *doesn't actually hold* in the general population. The signal you thought was there isn't.

/>

These are the failure modes validation is designed to catch. Bias and variance you diagnose by looking at the gap between training and test error. Overfit you confirm with cross-validation. Nonsignificance you check by comparing your model to a naive baseline and asking *is this difference even real?*

## Bias and variance — what they actually mean

The terms get used loosely in conversation. In this context they mean specific things.

### Variance — sensitivity to training data

A model with **high variance** gives wildly different predictions for slightly different inputs.

Example. You're predicting credit score. Someone aged 37, all other features average — your model says 0.7. A near-identical person aged 39 — same job, same income, same everything else — your model says 0.1. That huge swing for a tiny input change is variance. The model isn't learning the pattern; it's memorising the training points and panicking when the input doesn't match one exactly.

High variance is the signature of <KeyTerm>overfitting</KeyTerm>. The model has hugged the training data so tightly that it can't generalise. When it sees a new example, even one similar to what it trained on, it doesn't understand the pattern and gives weird predictions.

The visual: a regression curve that snakes through every training point individually. Training error is zero. Test error is awful.

### Bias — systematic under-fitting

The opposite. A model with **high bias** can't even fit the training data. It gives you a default — an average — instead of learning anything.

The visual: a straight horizontal line through clearly curved data. Or a linear regression on a relationship that's obviously non-linear. Training error is high; test error is also high (often similar to training).

### The trade-off

Make the model more complex → training error drops, variance grows. Make it simpler → variance drops, but bias grows. You can't drive both to zero at the same time. The job is to find the balance.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/bias-variance-tradeoff-card.png"
  alt="Bias-variance trade-off. Top three panels: Underfit (red straight line missing curved data, 'too simple to capture the pattern'); Good fit (green curve following the trend, 'captures the pattern and generalizes well'); Overfit (blue wavy line, 'too complex and learns the noise'). Bottom panel: prediction error vs model complexity. Training error (dark blue) decreases monotonically. Test error (green) is U-shaped — it drops from the high-bias region on the left, bottoms at the sweet spot in the middle, then rises in the high-variance region on the right. The sweet spot is annotated 'balanced'."
  caption="Training error always falls. Test error is U-shaped. The bottom of the U is the model you ship."
/>

The key picture: plot **training error** and **test error** against model complexity.

- **Training error** drops monotonically. Always. The more flexible the model, the better it can fit the training set.
- **Test error** is **U-shaped**. It drops at first (the model is learning the pattern), bottoms out, then rises (the model starts memorising noise).

The minimum of the test-error curve is the sweet spot. The training error tells you *nothing* about where this minimum is. **You can drive training error to zero with a complex-enough model — that doesn't mean it's good.**

<Callout color="yellow">
  💡 Training error always goes down. Test error is U-shaped. <Highlight>The trade-off between bias and variance is the entire game.</Highlight>
</Callout>

## Training error vs test error — the gap is the diagnosis

The relationship between training error and test error is itself the diagnostic.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/training-vs-test-error.png"
  alt="Training error vs test error. Left panel: a chart with model complexity on the x-axis and error on the y-axis. Training error (dark line) falls monotonically; test error (blue line) falls, hits a green 'best generalization' point at the bottom of its U-curve, then rises into a red 'overfitting' region. Right panel — how to read this: training error almost always falls as complexity rises; test error reveals generalization; choose the model near the test-error minimum; the gap between curves reflects overfitting risk. Bottom: 'best training score does not guarantee best real-world performance'."
  caption="Training error keeps falling — that's just the model getting more flexible. Test error tells you whether it's also getting useful."
/>

Read the picture:

- **Both errors are high.** Bias problem — the model is too simple. Make it more flexible.
- **Training error is low, test error is high.** Variance problem — overfitting. Simplify the model or add regularisation.
- **Both errors are low and similar.** Healthy fit. Ship it.
- **Both errors are flat and similar to a naive baseline.** Nonsignificance — the model isn't really learning. Check whether the features have any relationship to the target at all.

The gap between train and test is the signal. If they move together, the model is honest. If they diverge, the model is memorising.

## The problem with a single train/test split

The standard ML workflow you learn first:

1. Split your data 80/20.
2. Train the model on the 80%.
3. Evaluate on the 20%.
4. Report the test score.

This works — most of the time. The problem is the **randomness of the split**.

Imagine you're predicting Titanic survival. The split is random. What happens if, by chance, all the first-class passengers ended up in your training set and the test set is mostly third-class? Your model looks brilliant on train (the easy cases) and terrible on test. Not because the model is bad — because the split was *unlucky*.

The reverse is also true. With a lucky split — easy cases in test, hard ones in train — your model looks 90% accurate. You deploy it. In production: 75%.

  caption="Same model, same dataset — the random split alone can swing the reported accuracy by 10+ points."
/>

<Callout color="red">
  ⚠️ With the same model and the same dataset, just changing the random seed of the train/test split can change your reported score by 5-10 percentage points. <Highlight>A single split is a coin flip.</Highlight>
</Callout>

## Cross-validation — the fix

The solution: do the splitting many times, average the results.

This is <KeyTerm>k-fold cross-validation</KeyTerm>. The process:

1. Split your training data into `k` equal-sized folds (typically `k = 5` or `10`).
2. For each fold `i` in 1..k:
   - Train the model on the *other* k-1 folds.
   - Evaluate it on fold `i`.
3. Average the k scores.

By doing this k times, every data point gets to be in the validation set exactly once, and you're never tricked by a single lucky / unlucky split. The output is something like *"accuracy 85% ± 1%"* — the average score, plus the spread.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/cross-validation-card.png"
  alt="Cross-validation — a more stable estimate. Three panels. (1) Single hold-out: split once, get one validation score (0.72); 'one estimate only'. (2) Cross-validation: rotate the validation set across k=5 folds; each fold yields a score (0.71, 0.74, 0.73, 0.70, 0.75); 'multiple estimates'. (3) Average performance: the five fold scores averaged into one number (0.73, the mean of 5 folds); 'less dependent on luck'. Bottom row: use for model comparison, helps with limited data, still keep a final untouched test set. Bottom callout: 'cross-validation reduces the risk of over-trusting a lucky split'."
  caption="Each fold takes a turn being the validation set. The final number is the average across all k folds, so no single split can fool you."
/>

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=5)
print(f"{scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")
```

That `± 1%` is critical. It tells you how stable the model is. If the spread is huge (say, 60-95% across folds), the model is unstable and your headline accuracy is meaningless. If the spread is tight (84-86%), you can actually trust the number.

<Callout color="yellow">
  🔑 <strong>Cross-validation gives you the average performance of your model AND its variance.</strong> Both matter. Don't quote a single accuracy number — quote a range. <Highlight>"My model is 85% ± 1%"</Highlight> is honest. <Highlight>"My model is 85%"</Highlight> is hiding something.
</Callout>

### Why CV scores drop compared to single-split scores

The cross-validation score is usually lower than a one-shot train/test score. Reason: each CV fold only uses k-1/k of the data for training (say, 80% if k=5), so each individual model is trained on slightly less data than your final model will be.

That's fine. The CV score is the *honest estimate of generalisation*; the higher single-split score was probably an artefact of a lucky split.

## Cross-validation variants — k-fold, LOOCV, repeated k-fold

K-fold isn't the only resampling scheme. Different variants trade off computation, stability, and how much data each individual model gets to train on.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/cv-variants.png"
  alt="Cross-validation variants. Three options shown side by side. K-fold: five folds with one in green as the held-out validation set; split into k folds, each fold becomes validation once, balanced cost vs stability; 'common default'. LOOCV: a long row of single-point folds with one held out; one sample held out each time, uses almost all data for training, computationally expensive; 'maximizes training data'. Repeated k-fold: three rows of 5-fold splits with the held-out fold rotating; repeat k-fold with new random splits, reduces split luck further, useful when the dataset is small or noisy; 'more stable estimate'. Bottom table comparing computation, variance, and when each is useful — K-fold: medium/medium/good general choice; LOOCV: high/noisy/very small datasets; Repeated k-fold: higher/lower/when you want extra stability."
  caption="Three resampling schemes for the same goal: get a fair generalisation estimate. The trade-off is computation vs stability."
/>

- **K-fold.** The default. Split into `k = 5` or `k = 10` folds, rotate the validation fold. Balanced cost and stability. Use this unless you have a specific reason not to.
- **LOOCV** (Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation). The extreme case where `k = n` — each individual observation gets to be the validation set once. Maximises the training data each model sees, but the fold scores can be noisy and the cost is high (you fit the model `n` times). Useful when the dataset is very small.
- **Repeated k-fold.** Run k-fold multiple times with different random splits, average across all of them. Reduces the dependence on which particular folds you happened to draw. Useful when the dataset is small or noisy and you want a more stable estimate.

In practice: start with 5-fold or 10-fold. Reach for repeated k-fold if the fold-to-fold variance is uncomfortably large. Reach for LOOCV only on small datasets where you can afford the computation.

## The 3-way split — train, validation, test

The next subtlety. Suppose you're choosing between Ridge and Lasso, and you also want to find the optimal `λ` for each.

You can't just train each version and check the test set, because:

1. You're going to evaluate 25 values of `λ` for Ridge, 25 for Lasso → 50 model variants.
2. If you score all 50 on the test set and pick the best, **you've selected against the test set**. Your test score is now contaminated — it's not measuring generalisation, it's measuring which model happens to fit your specific test split best.

The fix: split into **three** sets.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/train-validation-test-sets.png"
  alt="Train, validation, and test sets. A 60/20/20 split shown as three coloured bars. Training set (60%, green): fit the model, learn parameters. Validation set (20%, blue): compare models, tune hyperparameters, select the best setup. Test set (20%, red): use once at the end, estimate real-world generalization; warning 'never tune on the test set'. Bottom row: three workflow boxes — Train (training data → model, learn patterns and fit parameters), Validate (validation data → choose between Model A/B/C → best setup, compare performance and choose the best), Test (test data unseen → final model → ✓, evaluate once to estimate real-world performance). Bottom callout: 'if you use the test set for selection, it stops being unseen'."
  caption="The 60/20/20 split. The test set has one job — final check — and it does that job exactly once."
/>

1. **Training set** — fit candidate models.
2. **Validation set** — pick the best one. Try as many configurations as you want. The validation set is where hyperparameter tuning happens.
3. **Test set** — the final, one-shot honest measurement. You touch it once at the end, after you've picked the final model. Never touch it again.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/validation-workflow.png"
  alt="Validation workflow — tune here, test once at the end. Three columns. (1) Training: dataset 'Train' highlighted; fit model parameters, learn from examples, try candidate model families; 'learning stage'. (2) Validation: dataset 'Validation' highlighted; compare model choices, tune hyperparameters, pick the best setup; 'model selection happens here'. (3) Final test: dataset 'Test' highlighted with a padlock; evaluate once at the end, estimate real-world performance, do not use for repeated tuning; 'keep this untouched'. Why this matters: repeated use of the test set leaks information; validation protects model selection; final test keeps the estimate honest. Bottom: 'Choose on validation. Report on test.'"
  caption="Three datasets, three jobs. The most important rule: the test set is not a knob you turn — it's a verdict you receive."
/>

In practice you usually combine train + validation into the CV pool (so the validation step *is* k-fold CV on the training data), and the test set is genuinely held out from start to finish.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, GridSearchCV

# 1. Carve out test set first — never look at it during selection
X_dev, X_test, y_dev, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2)

# 2. Tune hyperparameters via CV on the dev set
grid = GridSearchCV(model, param_grid={'alpha': [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}, cv=5)
grid.fit(X_dev, y_dev)

# 3. ONE final measurement on the test set
final_score = grid.score(X_test, y_test)
```

<Callout color="red">
  ⚠️ Every time you peek at the test set during model selection, you contaminate it. The test set is your only honest measure of generalisation — <Highlight>spend it once</Highlight>.
</Callout>

## After model selection — retrain on all the data

A subtle step that's easy to skip. Once cross-validation has told you which hyperparameter setting is best, **you don't ship the model from the best fold.** You ship a freshly-trained model that uses *all* the available training data with those hyperparameters.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/retrain-after-selection.png"
  alt="After model selection, retrain on all training data. Three columns. (1) Compare candidates: Model A (0.74), Model B (0.77, highlighted as best), Model C (0.73) shown with validation scores; 'pick the best hyperparameters / setup'. (2) Retrain the winner: train + validation combined and used to fit the final model; combine all non-test data, refit the chosen model, learn from as much data as possible; 'final training fit'. (3) Test once: final model evaluated against an untouched test set; use untouched test set, report final metric, do not tune again after this; 'no more iteration on the test set'. Why retrain: cross-validation helps choose the configuration, the final model should use all available development data, the test set remains the last independent check. Bottom: 'Select with validation, retrain on all development data, then test once.'"
  caption="Cross-validation chooses the configuration. The final model is a fresh fit on all the development data at that configuration."
/>

The logic: cross-validation gave you an *estimate* of how well a given configuration generalises. The actual model you trained inside each fold was on a smaller subset of the data. Now that you've decided which configuration to ship, you let it learn from everything you've got — that gives you the best model the data can produce. Then you measure it once on the untouched test set.

The mental model is:

1. Use cross-validation on (train + validation) to **pick the configuration**.
2. Refit the chosen configuration on **all of (train + validation)**.
3. Measure once on **test**.
4. Ship.

Step 2 is the one beginners forget. The model that goes into production is *not* one of the fold-models; it's a final fit on the whole non-test dataset.

## Bootstrap — for small samples and confidence intervals

What if your dataset is too small to even do cross-validation reliably? Or you want a confidence interval around a summary statistic — the mean, the median, the accuracy — without making distributional assumptions?

That's where the <KeyTerm>bootstrap</KeyTerm> comes in. The idea: instead of theorising about what the sampling distribution looks like, **simulate it** by resampling from the data you have.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/bootstrapping-card.png"
  alt="Bootstrapping. Three numbered panels. (1) Original sample: a row of 10 numbered items labelled 'one dataset'. (2) Sample with replacement: three example bootstrap samples drawn from the original, each the same size but with repeated numbers (e.g. bootstrap 1: 3,7,2,9,1,5,2,6,7); 'same sample size, but repeated observations are allowed'; badge 'resampling with replacement'. (3) Estimate variability: each bootstrap sample yields a score (0.71, 0.75, 0.73, 0.70, 0.74, …); a distribution histogram with an uncertainty / confidence-interval band; 'measure how results vary, useful when the dataset is small, helps quantify confidence in the score'; badge 'more than one number'. Two badges at the bottom: ✓ good for estimating uncertainty (uses the data you have to understand how much results might vary by chance), ✗ not the same as creating new information (it reuses existing observations; it doesn't add new data or fix bias). Bottom: 'Bootstrapping asks: if we had sampled again, how much might the result change?'"
  caption="Bootstrap simulates many alternative datasets by resampling the one you have with replacement. The spread across resamples is your uncertainty."
/>

The recipe, from my notes:

1. Start with your sample — say, 5 measurements: $[1, 2, 4, 4, 10]$.
2. Generate a new "bootstrap sample" by drawing 5 elements **with replacement** from the original. (Repeats are fine — the same number can appear twice or not at all.)
3. Compute the statistic you care about on this bootstrap sample (the mean, the model's accuracy, whatever).
4. Repeat 2-3 thousands of times. Each repetition gives you one value of the statistic.
5. The distribution of those values is the **bootstrap distribution** of your statistic. The 5th and 95th percentiles give you a 90% confidence interval. The standard deviation gives you a standard error.

When to reach for bootstrap:

- **Small sample size** (typically n < 40-50) — too little data to fit a parametric distribution.
- **Estimating a confidence interval** around any summary statistic.
- **Quantifying uncertainty** in a score without making assumptions about its distribution.

What bootstrap is *not*: it's not creating new information. It uses the data you have to estimate how variable your results are across *plausible alternative versions* of that same data. If your sample is biased, bootstrap will give you a confident estimate of the *biased* answer. Garbage in, confident-looking garbage out.

## Significance testing — is the improvement real?

You ran two models. Model A got accuracy 0.78. Model B got 0.79. Is B better?

Maybe. Maybe it's just noise — variance in the cross-validation folds, randomness in the train/test split, lucky sampling. A higher number alone isn't proof. You need evidence that the difference is *unlikely to have happened by chance*.

That's what <KeyTerm>significance testing</KeyTerm> does. The traditional framing uses a <KeyTerm>p-value</KeyTerm>:

- Set up the **null hypothesis** — "there's no real difference between A and B; the observed gap is random."
- Compute the probability of seeing a gap *at least this big* if the null were true. That's the p-value.
- If the p-value is small (commonly < 0.05), you reject the null — there's evidence the difference is real.
- If it's large, you can't distinguish the observed gap from chance.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/significance-testing-card.png"
  alt="Significance testing — is the model really better? Three numbered panels. (1) Compare against a baseline: Your model 0.78 vs Naive baseline 0.74; compare to a simple reference, ask whether the gap is meaningful, accuracy alone is not the whole story; 'better than naive?'. (2) Variation matters: violin plots of Your model (0.78, green) and Naive baseline (0.74, blue) showing distributions; results vary from sample to sample, small gaps may be noise, uncertainty must be considered; warning 'don't trust tiny differences blindly'. (3) Decision: 'is the difference statistically significant?' decision tree branching into 'difference is statistically meaningful' (green check, evidence the model improves on baseline) vs 'difference is not significant' (red X, may be luck / nonsignificance); 'use evidence, not excitement'. Bottom: 'The goal is not just a bigger metric, but confidence that the improvement is real. A model should beat both the baseline and random fluctuation.'"
  caption="A bigger number isn't a better model. The improvement has to beat the noise — and a naive baseline."
/>

In ML practice, two specific moves matter:

- **Compare against a naive baseline.** Before you celebrate 78% accuracy, ask: what does a model that always predicts the majority class score? What does a model that picks at random get? If "your" model is barely better than these, the improvement might be nonsignificance.
- **Look at the spread, not just the mean.** Cross-validation gives you `0.78 ± 0.03`. The naive baseline gives you `0.74 ± 0.04`. The means differ by 0.04 — but the spreads overlap. Is that "significant"? Run a t-test on the per-fold scores and find out.

The lesson, in one sentence: **a higher score is not enough; you want evidence the improvement isn't random fluctuation.**

## Distribution drift — what the test set can't catch

One thing the test set can't catch, no matter how careful your validation: **the production data changing over time**.

<Image
  src="/images/blog/ml-from-scratch/evaluation-metrics/distribution-drift-card.png"
  alt="Distribution drift. Left panel — Training-time world: a 2D scatter plot with three clean clusters (blue, red, green); historical data; model learned from this distribution, assumptions fit the past, evaluation looked good; badge 'old pattern'. Right panel — Production-time world: similar feature space but the clusters have shifted — red cluster has grown, green cluster has shrunk; real inputs changed, target behavior may change too, old model can degrade; badge 'new pattern'. Between them: an arrow labelled 'time'. Middle panel — What drift causes: lower accuracy / reliability, thresholds may stop working well, retraining or monitoring becomes necessary, business assumptions may no longer hold. Bottom — Common examples: user behavior changes, seasonality, policy/market shifts. Bottom callout: 'Good validation on past data does not guarantee future stability.'"
  caption="The model trained on yesterday's distribution. Today's distribution might not be the same one. Drift is the slow, silent way models fail in production."
/>

Most ML methods assume that the data distribution is **stationary** — meaning, what the model trained on is what it'll see at inference. In practice, distributions change. New customer demographics, new product categories, seasonality, regulatory shifts, a competitor that just launched.

When the production distribution drifts away from the training distribution, the model's accuracy degrades. Sometimes slowly, sometimes overnight. The model didn't get worse — *the world changed under it*.

What drift causes:

- **Lower accuracy and reliability** on the metric you trained for.
- **Thresholds stop working.** A spam threshold tuned for last year's spam doesn't catch this year's.
- **Business assumptions break.** A churn model that learned "customers who don't log in for 30 days will leave" stops working when product behaviour changes.

Common drivers:

- **User behaviour changes** — products, interactions, demographics.
- **Seasonality** — holiday traffic, end-of-quarter effects, weather.
- **Policy or market shifts** — a regulation, a competitor, a recession.

The fix is **monitoring**: track the model's metric in production, retrain when it drops. We won't go deep here, but it's worth knowing the term and the discipline. It's the reason every production ML system needs retraining infrastructure — *the model is never done; it's just current.*

## The honest workflow, version 2

A unified picture of how to actually evaluate and validate a model in practice:

<Callout color="green">
  1. <strong>Pick your metric before you train</strong> based on the cost of mistakes ([Part 4](/blog/ml-from-scratch-04-classification-metrics)).<br/>
  2. <strong>Diagnose the failure mode</strong>: bias, variance, overfit, or nonsignificance. Each has a different fix.<br/>
  3. <strong>Carve out a test set</strong> at the start and never touch it.<br/>
  4. <strong>Do hyperparameter selection with k-fold CV</strong> on the remaining data. Report the mean ± std, not a single number.<br/>
  5. <strong>Pick the model at the bottom of the U-curve</strong>. Smallest test error, not smallest training error.<br/>
  6. <strong>Retrain the chosen configuration on all the development data</strong> before shipping.<br/>
  7. <strong>Bootstrap</strong> when the sample is too small for stable CV, or when you need a confidence interval.<br/>
  8. <strong>Significance-test</strong> against a naive baseline. A higher score isn't evidence — beating noise is.<br/>
  9. <strong>Touch the test set once</strong> for a final, honest score.<br/>
  10. <strong>Monitor in production</strong> — distributions drift; retrain when they do.
</Callout>
